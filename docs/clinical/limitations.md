# CardioGuard AI — Clinical Limitations & Scope of Use

> [!CAUTION]
> **LEGAL AND CLINICAL WARNING**:  
> CardioGuard AI is an **experimental, research-grade software platform** developed for academic, educational, and developer evaluation purposes. It is **NOT** a certified Medical Device under US FDA 21 CFR Part 820 / 510(k), European Union Medical Device Regulation (EU MDR 2017/745), or the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) of India.

---

## 1. What CardioGuard AI Is NOT

1. **NOT a Medical Diagnostic Device**: CardioGuard AI cannot make a definitive medical diagnosis of hypertension, acute myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, cardiac arrhythmia, or heart failure.
2. **NOT a Real-Time Patient Monitor**: The platform does not provide continuous telemetry, intensive care unit (ICU) grade monitoring, or automated life-support triggers.
3. **NOT a Replacement for Clinical Judgement**: Recommendations, risk estimates, and summaries generated by the application must never replace direct examination, auscultation, clinical history-taking, and professional judgment by a licensed medical practitioner.
4. **NOT Capable of Ruling Out Acute Events**: Normal vital signs, a low Framingham risk score, or an unflagged ECG tracing **do not guarantee the absence of severe cardiovascular disease or acute coronary syndrome**.

---

## 2. Inherent Algorithmic & Technological Limitations

### 2.1 Consumer Sensor Inaccuracy
- Measurements logged into the system frequently originate from consumer-grade photoplethysmography (PPG) smartwatches or oscillometric upper-arm/wrist cuffs.
- These devices are prone to motion artifact, poor skin contact, peripheral vasoconstriction, and calibration drift.
- The system cannot verify hardware sensor accuracy and must assume user input may be noisy or inaccurate.

### 2.2 Population Specificity of Epidemiological Models
- The Framingham Risk Calculator was derived on an American cohort between 1971 and 2008.
- It exhibits documented under-prediction of cardiovascular event risk in individuals of South Asian ancestry, individuals with chronic inflammatory diseases, and individuals with severe family histories of premature CAD.

### 2.3 Generative Language Model Uncertainty
- The conversational health assistant utilizes large language models (LLMs).
- Despite hardcoded safety rules, regex filters, and prompt boundaries, LLMs remain probabilistic neural networks capable of syntactic hallucination, subtle medical misunderstandings, or inappropriate tone if safety boundaries are bypassed.

## 3. Clear Boundaries of Safe Use

| Allowed Scenarios (In Scope) | Prohibited Scenarios (Out of Scope) |
|---|---|
| Historical logging of blood pressure and resting heart rate for personal review. | Relying on the app during active, crushing chest pain or shortness of breath instead of calling emergency services. |
| Reviewing educational materials regarding dietary sodium and cardiovascular wellness. | Adjusting prescribed prescription medications (e.g., beta-blockers, statins, ACE inhibitors) based on app scores. |
| Sharing longitudinal vital history charts with a licensed physician during an office visit. | Using the platform as an automated triage tool in an emergency department or clinic. |
| Academic experimentation with signal processing algorithms on open datasets. | Using unvalidated ECG processing to diagnose cardiac arrhythmias in clinical patients. |

---

## 4. Required Steps Before Real-World Clinical Deployment

Before any deployment involving real patients in a clinical setting:
1. Formal clinical trial registration (e.g., CTRI / ClinicalTrials.gov) and ethics committee review.
2. Rigorous multicenter clinical validation demonstrating non-inferiority to standard triage protocols.
3. Regulatory submission and clearance as a Software-as-a-Medical-Device (SaMD).
4. Establishment of a formal Quality Management System (ISO 13485) and Medical Device Post-Market Surveillance program.
